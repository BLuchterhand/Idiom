## Chapter Claim

Trustworthy AI adoption has to show who proposes, who checks, who can reject,
who pays for review, and who owns the downside when a fast answer is wrong.

## Reader Starting Point

The reader may believe naming an accountable person is enough.

## First Visible Moment

An ownership meeting names the support manager accountable for the customer
answer. The manager has no finance authority, no compliance backing, no reviewer
budget, and no rejection authority.

Scene beats:

1. The meeting starts with a clean RACI-style answer: support owns the customer.
2. Finance asks who owns the credit exposure; compliance asks who owns fairness.
3. Sales asks why review is blocking the renewal conversation.
4. The support manager realizes accountability has been assigned without
   matching authority.
5. The map shows whose incentive improves when the AI answer moves fast and
   whose risk grows when it is wrong.

## Workplace Behavior Parallel

Organizations often name owners for decisions without giving them the rights
needed to make those decisions safely.

## Human Fallibility Pattern

People accept blame paths faster than they clarify decision rights. Speed
beneficiaries and risk owners are often different people.

## Existing Workplace Contract

Real accountability includes evidence access, authority to reject, budget or
capacity, escalation path, and political cover.

## AI Analogue

AI can generate work faster than the organization can decide who pays review
cost, who owns downside risk, and who can veto an attractive answer.

## Analogy Break

AI expands proposal volume and makes missing authority easier to overlook: a
human name appears in the loop even when the workflow gives that human no
practical control.

## Operator Artifact

Responsibility and incentive map:

| Role | Wants | Pays Cost | Owns Risk | Veto Right | Escalation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sales | Speed, retention | Low | Renewal risk | No | Executive sponsor |
| Support | Closure | Medium | Customer message | Weak | Support lead |
| Finance | Budget control | Review time | Credit exposure | Yes | CFO path |
| Compliance | Consistency | Review time | Fairness/audit | Yes | Legal path |

Does not own: reviewer-interface design, capacity economics, or governance
learning loops. This chapter owns decision rights and incentive pressure.

Artifact validation:

| Field | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Owner | The executive sponsor owns resolving incentive conflicts; domain owners own their authority boundaries. |
| Source authority | Authority comes from approval policy, budget rules, compliance obligations, and escalation policy. |
| Update cadence | Review after blocked decisions, overrides, or repeated reviewer pressure. |
| Failure signal | Named owners lack evidence, rejection authority, budget, escalation path, or escalation cover for delay. |
| Operational hook | Escalate, reduce what the output may do before acceptance, fund review capacity, or halt acceptance until authority matches accountability. |
| Escalation/retirement | If an owner cannot reject safely, escalate or reduce what the output may do before acceptance. |

## Main Sections

1. **The ownership meeting**: show accountability without control.
2. **Three kinds of work**: model strategy, workflow contracts, human judgment.
3. **The incentive map**: speed, cost, risk, veto, blame.
4. **What accountable owners need**: reject, halt, fund, escalate, change.
5. **Rejection authority**: make rejection operationally safe.

## Practical Takeaway

Do not name an accountable owner unless the workflow also gives that owner
evidence, rejection authority, escalation, budget or capacity, and escalation
cover for delay.

## Possible Objections

- "This is politics": yes, adoption is political when incentives diverge.
- "We cannot give every reviewer rejection authority": scale rejection authority by consequence.
- "The model can decide": the model can propose; people and systems accept.
