## Chapter Claim

AI can produce useful judgment-like work without owning the authority to decide
whether that work is true, current, permitted, or accepted.

## Reader Starting Point

The reader may describe AI as a new employee, a search engine, an automation
script, or "just autocomplete." Each frame catches part of the truth and misses
the adoption problem.

## First Visible Moment

An analyst writes a polished recommendation from incomplete notes. The memo is
clear and domain-fluent, but it cites no current policy, does not verify the
contract, and quietly assumes finance authority.

Scene beats:

1. The analyst's memo is useful enough that nobody wants to throw it away.
2. A manager notices the memo names the right customer and policy family but
   lacks the current source.
3. Finance asks who approved the dollar amount; nobody in the memo can answer.
4. The team labels the memo as proposal, not decision, and routes it through
   source and authority checks.
5. The AI analogue enters only after the reader recognizes the ordinary memo
   failure.

## Workplace Behavior Parallel

Professional readers know that a confident memo is not a decision. A memo can
be useful work and still require source review, authority checks, and acceptance.

## Human Fallibility Pattern

Fluency, domain vocabulary, and confidence can make incomplete work feel more
finished than it is.

## Existing Workplace Contract

Decision memos get checked against sources, authority, decision rights, and
review criteria before they may change customers, money, records, or
system state.

## AI Analogue

LLMs can synthesize context, generalize patterns, translate intent, draft plans,
and use tools. Their capability does not automatically carry truth, freshness,
permission, or accountable acceptance.

## Analogy Break

AI can generate polished output at scale, blend learned patterns with current
context, and hide uncertainty behind consistent prose. It cannot be accountable
in the human sense.

## Operator Artifact

Output state label:

| Output | Current State | Required Promotion Step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Summary | Draft | Source spot-check |
| Recommendation | Proposal | Evidence and authority review |
| Customer communication | Not permitted | Accepted decision and communication owner |
| System change | Not permitted | Permission boundary and post-action verification |

Artifact validation:

| Field | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Owner | The workflow owner maintains output states and promotion steps. |
| Source authority | Promotion depends on the source map and authority rules introduced in later chapters. |
| Update cadence | Review when the workflow adds a new output type or changes what the output may do before acceptance. |
| Failure signal | People act on draft/proposal output as if it were accepted. |
| Operational hook | Block promotion to customer, money, record, or system effects until the required promotion step completes. |
| Escalation/retirement | If labels are ignored, block the effect rather than rely on visible labeling alone. |

## Practical Takeaway

Before acting on AI output, label its state: draft, proposal, ready for review,
accepted, or not permitted.

## Possible Objections

- "This sounds like downplaying AI intelligence": answer by respecting
  capability while separating it from authority.
- "Humans also lack perfect authority": answer by using the same promotion
  pattern for human work.
- "Better models will fix this": answer that better models move the boundary
  and expand delegation.
